Steven M. Grimes, PhD, Spent More Than Five Decades Measuring Neutrons and Teaching Others How

The Ohio University Distinguished Professor Emeritus moved from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory to Athens, where he directed the Edwards Accelerator Laboratory and kept publishing nuclear research well into the 2020s.

Ohio UniversityAthens, OHAugust 31, 2026

ATHENS, Ohio. Steven M. Grimes first learned to think about physical systems at home. His father was an engineer, and when the family's house was being designed the talk turned to heating, cooling and electrical systems. The curiosity those conversations sparked eventually led him to the atomic nucleus, a subject he has studied for more than half a century.

His formal path began at Stanford University, where he earned a bachelor's degree in 1963. He went on to the University of Wisconsin for graduate work, completing a master's degree in 1964 and a doctorate in 1968. Along the way he was elected to Sigma Xi and Phi Beta Kappa.

A year of postdoctoral research at the University of Basel in Switzerland came next, followed by a second postdoctoral year at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California. Livermore then hired him as a staff physicist, and he stayed from 1971 to 1981, working alongside the specialized facilities and multidisciplinary teams of a major national laboratory.

Ohio University entered the picture in 1978, when he started teaching there as a visiting professor. Three years later he left Livermore and moved to Athens full time as a professor of physics, adding the training of young scientists to his research.

Much of his Ohio career centered on the Edwards Accelerator Laboratory, which he served as a researcher and, through 1991, as director. Built around a 4.5 MV tandem accelerator, the laboratory supports experiments in nuclear structure, nuclear astrophysics and applied nuclear physics, as well as the production and measurement of neutrons. University technical documents still credit him with contributing to its neutron source capabilities.

Neutrons have been the steady thread in his work. His papers deal with nuclear level densities, collisions between neutrons and nuclei, neutron scattering measurements and the energy spectra of neutrons released in fission. That research matters for basic physics and also for nuclear energy, detector calibration and astrophysics. In 2021 he and Nikolay V. Kornilov published a paper in Annals of Nuclear Energy on prompt fission neutron spectra and the persistent gap between microscopic and macroscopic measurements.

He has also helped shape how the field shares what it knows. He edited "Moment Methods" in 1979 and "Neutron Nucleus Collisions" in 1985, collections that brought specialized results together for other researchers. In 2008 he was honored as an Outstanding Referee, a recognition of years spent judging other scientists' work before it reached print.

Ohio University named him a Distinguished Professor in 2001, and he became Distinguished Professor Emeritus in 2011. His ties to the national laboratories never lapsed: he has worked as a consultant for both Lawrence Livermore and Los Alamos National Laboratory.

He is a Fellow of the American Physical Society, a distinction the society reserves for members whose research, applications or service have meaningfully advanced physics. Nuclear astrophysics, the study of how nuclear reactions power stars and forge the elements, is among the areas most closely tied to his expertise.

Dynamo Times recognizes Steven M. Grimes, PhD, for more than five decades of experimental nuclear physics, for leadership of the Edwards Accelerator Laboratory and for the generations of physicists he helped train at Ohio University.

About Ohio University

Steven M. Grimes, PhD, is Distinguished Professor Emeritus of physics at Ohio University, where he taught from 1978 and directed the Edwards Accelerator Laboratory through 1991. He holds a bachelor's degree from Stanford University and master's and doctoral degrees from the University of Wisconsin, and he worked as a physicist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory from 1971 to 1981. A Fellow of the American Physical Society, he has consulted for both Lawrence Livermore and Los Alamos National Laboratory.

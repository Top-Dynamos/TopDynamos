Ronald E. Berenbeim, JD, Builds a Global Legacy in Business Ethics, Corporate Responsibility and Anti-Corruption Education

Attorney, researcher and NYU Stern educator Ronald E. Berenbeim has spent decades examining corporate ethics, governance and anti-corruption through The Conference Board, the World Bank and international management education initiatives.

The Conference Board / NYU Stern School of BusinessNew York, NYSeptember 22, 2026

Dynamo Times highlights Ronald E. Berenbeim, JD, an attorney, researcher, author and longtime educator whose professional career has been devoted to the study and teaching of business ethics, corporate responsibility, governance and anti-corruption.

Berenbeim's career has connected the corporate world with universities, international organizations, public-policy institutions and nonprofit initiatives. His professional history includes decades of work with The Conference Board, teaching at New York University's Stern School of Business, research associated with the World Bank, participation in United Nations anti-corruption education initiatives and scholarship examining the responsibilities of corporate boards and business leaders. A 2022 Marquis Who's Who profile recognized Berenbeim with inclusion in Who's Who in the World for his work in business ethics.

Berenbeim's interest in ethics developed while he was an undergraduate at Cornell University. According to his biographical profile, he studied with political theorist Hannah Arendt, an experience that helped deepen his interest in questions of responsibility, judgment and ethical conduct. He graduated from Cornell in 1966 with a BA, cum laude. He then received a Keasbey Memorial Scholarship to study at Balliol College, Oxford.

At Oxford, Berenbeim studied modern history and attended lectures by philosopher and historian of ideas Isaiah Berlin. He earned a BA from Oxford in 1968. His time there placed him in an intellectual environment concerned with history, politics, liberty and the difficult choices institutions and individuals make. Those questions would later reappear in a different setting: corporate decision-making.

After Oxford, Berenbeim attended Harvard Law School, earning his JD in 1971. Legal education added another dimension to his developing interest in ethics. Law establishes formal rules and enforceable standards. Ethics asks what organizations and individuals ought to do, including in situations where the law may not provide a complete answer. Much of Berenbeim's later career would examine the relationship between these two systems of responsibility.

During the summer of 1969, while attending Harvard, Berenbeim participated in the early consumer-advocacy movement associated with Ralph Nader. His 2022 profile states that he was part of the first wave of "Nader's Raiders" and contributed to The Interstate Commerce Omission. He also testified before a congressional committee concerning the book's findings. The experience exposed him early to questions about institutions, regulation, accountability and the public interest.

After law school, Berenbeim began his professional career as an attorney with the National Labor Relations Board in Seattle. He subsequently worked as a business representative for the Motion Picture and Television Cameramen's Union. These roles provided direct experience with labor law, organizational relationships and negotiations. They also preceded the work in corporate ethics that would eventually define much of his career.

From 1977 through 1988, Berenbeim served as a non-executive director of Guaranty National Corporation, according to his Marquis profile. Board service gave him firsthand exposure to corporate governance. That experience would become particularly relevant as his research increasingly examined how boards oversee ethics and compliance programs. Corporate ethics is not solely an employee-level issue. Board members and senior executives influence organizational culture through policies, incentives, oversight and their own conduct.

In 1977, Berenbeim joined The Conference Board, an independent business research organization. His relationship with the organization would span decades. He developed and expanded its business-ethics program and served in research, conference leadership and networking roles. The Conference Board's current archived biography identifies him as a former Senior Fellow and documents his work in business ethics and anti-corruption.

Berenbeim became a prolific researcher on corporate ethics. His 2022 Marquis profile credits him with 55 Conference Board publications. His work examined topics including corporate codes of conduct, ethics programs, board oversight, corruption, third-party risk and responsible business practices. The research followed the evolution of corporate ethics from a relatively specialized management topic into an important area of board and executive oversight.

In 2006, The Conference Board published Berenbeim's report "Ethics Programs and Practices: A 20-Year Retrospective." The report analyzed changes in corporate ethics and compliance programs over two decades. Among its findings, The Conference Board reported a dramatic increase in board involvement in formal ethics processes. The work documented how ethics programs were becoming more structured, more global and more closely connected to corporate governance.

Berenbeim repeatedly studied the responsibilities of boards in corporate ethics. His Conference Board publications include "Ethics Programs: The Role of the Board," "Ethics Programs ... The Role of the Board: A Global Study," co-authored with Jeffrey M. Kaplan, and "Ethics Issues and Programs: The Role of the Board." These reports examined how directors oversee ethics systems and how organizations address the conduct of board members themselves. The research helped frame ethics as a governance responsibility rather than simply a compliance department function.

In 2002, The Conference Board published Berenbeim's "The Enron Ethics Breakdown." The report examined ethical issues raised by the conduct of Enron's directors, officers, accountants and lawyers. Enron became one of the defining corporate scandals of its era. For business-ethics researchers, the case raised fundamental questions about conflicts of interest, professional responsibility, oversight and organizational culture.

Berenbeim also examined the relationship between formal ethics programs and corporate social responsibility. His 2005 Conference Board report "Defining the Corporate Ethics Brand" distinguished traditional ethics and compliance systems from corporate social-responsibility programs while examining how the two can shape an organization's reputation. The research recognized that stakeholders often evaluate a company not only by what it sells, but also by how it behaves.

In 1995, Berenbeim joined the faculty of New York University's Stern School of Business as an adjunct professor. NYU Stern's faculty biography identifies him as an Adjunct Professor of Business Ethics and states that he has taught Professional Responsibility: Markets, Ethics, and Law since 1995. His 2022 profile explains that he worked with other Stern faculty members to design a course integrating concepts from markets, ethics and law. That work helped provide the foundation for Stern's professional-responsibility curriculum.

Teaching ethics in a business school involves more than explaining rules. Future executives may face decisions in which financial incentives, legal obligations, stakeholder expectations and personal values point in different directions. A professional-responsibility course gives students a structured environment for examining those conflicts before encountering them in positions of authority. Berenbeim's teaching has therefore complemented his corporate research.

From 2001 to 2003, Berenbeim served as a project director for a World Bank study of private-sector anti-corruption practices in East Asia. He co-authored, with Jean-François Arvis, "Fighting Corruption in East Asia: Solutions from the Private Sector," published by the World Bank in 2003. The work examined how businesses can respond to corruption risks and how private-sector practices can contribute to broader anti-corruption efforts. Chinese and Vietnamese editions were subsequently published.

Berenbeim's work with The Conference Board and World Bank also extended to global supply chains. The Conference Board's biography identifies him as director of a joint project examining trade competitiveness and the integration of poor countries into global supply chains from the perspective of suppliers and producers. Global supply chains create economic opportunities, but they also raise questions about labor, governance, corruption and accountability across jurisdictions.

Berenbeim served as a co-facilitator for the United Nations Global Compact Principles for Responsible Management Education Anti-Corruption Working Group. The initiative brought business-school faculty together around the challenge of incorporating anti-corruption education into management curricula. He edited and contributed to "Anti-Corruption: Implementing Curriculum Change in Management Education," published in 2015. The work extended anti-corruption research from corporate practice into business education.

Berenbeim's teaching has reached beyond the United States. He received a Fulbright grant in 2010 to teach business ethics and governance at the University of Cergy-Pontoise in France. His 2022 profile also documents business-ethics and anti-corruption teaching at ISCTE in Lisbon in 2014 and IAE/IASE in Buenos Aires in 2016. These international teaching experiences reflect the global nature of modern business ethics.

In 2019, Berenbeim taught business ethics to graduating high-school students participating in a research program for arts and sciences at Donghua University in Shanghai, according to his Marquis profile. Teaching ethics before students enter universities or careers introduces the subject as a fundamental part of decision-making rather than a corrective measure introduced only after problems occur.

Berenbeim has also participated in organizations concerned with transparency and responsible business. His professional biographies document involvement with Transparency International's Steering Committee on Business Principles for Resisting Corrupt Practices, the United Nations Global Compact anti-corruption initiative and the U.S. advisory board of FTSE4Good. These activities placed his research within broader international efforts to improve corporate conduct.

Berenbeim's 2022 profile states that he secured research grants for Conference Board projects from the John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation and the World Bank in 2001 and 2008. Research support allows complex business issues to be studied systematically. Corporate ethics questions often require data from many organizations and jurisdictions rather than isolated anecdotes.

In 2010 and 2011, Berenbeim was selected by Trust Across America for its Top 100 Thought Leaders in Trustworthy Business Behavior recognition, according to his Marquis, NYU Stern and Conference Board biographies. The recognition reflected his longstanding work on trust, ethics and responsible organizational behavior. It should be understood as an award from the issuing organization rather than as a universal ranking of business-ethics scholars.

Berenbeim's career has consistently explored the territory between what organizations can do and what they should do. Law establishes minimum obligations and formal consequences. Ethics can require leaders to consider responsibilities that extend beyond compliance. Corporate governance determines who has authority and oversight. Markets create incentives. Professional responsibility asks individuals to navigate all of these forces simultaneously. His work has repeatedly brought these disciplines into conversation with one another.

Global business makes ethical decision-making more complicated. Companies may operate across countries with different legal systems, cultural expectations and enforcement environments. Suppliers and third parties may create risks far from corporate headquarters. Anti-corruption policies therefore need to function across borders. Berenbeim's research and teaching have addressed precisely these challenges.

Across his career, Berenbeim has worked as an attorney, corporate director, researcher, educator, author and international adviser. He helped develop The Conference Board's ethics research program. He contributed to World Bank anti-corruption research. He taught generations of students at NYU Stern and internationally. He participated in efforts to bring anti-corruption education into business-school curricula.

Dynamo Times recognizes Ronald E. Berenbeim, JD, for his longstanding contributions to business ethics, corporate governance, anti-corruption research, professional responsibility and global management education.

About The Conference Board / NYU Stern School of Business

Ronald E. Berenbeim, JD, is an attorney, researcher, author and business-ethics educator. He graduated cum laude from Cornell University, studied at Balliol College, Oxford as a Keasbey Scholar and earned his JD from Harvard Law School in 1971. His career includes service with the National Labor Relations Board, decades of research and leadership at The Conference Board, teaching at NYU Stern School of Business, World Bank anti-corruption research and participation in international responsible-management initiatives. In 2025, Children of Vietnam announced that Berenbeim had joined its Board of Directors, describing the role as a continuation of his longstanding commitment to ethical leadership, education and social justice.

Leanne Katz-Levin, PhD, Advances Patient-Centered Health Care Through Psychology, Education and Chronic Illness Research

New York Medical College patient-care leader and clinical psychologist Leanne Katz-Levin bridges health psychology, neuropsychology, chronic illness management, caregiver support and trauma-informed medical education.

New York Medical CollegeNew York, NYSeptember 22, 2026

Dynamo Times highlights Leanne Katz-Levin, PhD, a health care educator, clinical psychologist, researcher and patient-care leader whose career has focused on the human dimensions of medicine.

Dr. Katz-Levin currently serves as Director of Patient Care for the School of Medicine at New York Medical College and as an Assistant Professor in the Department of Medicine, according to her professional profile. Her work bridges health psychology, neuropsychology, chronic illness management, caregiver support, psychological assessment, trauma-informed care, research and medical education. Across these areas, a consistent principle emerges: effective health care requires patients to feel heard, understood and respected.

Chronic illness rarely affects only one dimension of a person's life. Pain can influence mood and daily functioning. Neurological conditions can affect cognition and independence. Cardiac and pulmonary disease can create psychological stress. Caregivers can face substantial burdens of their own. Dr. Katz-Levin's career has centered on these intersections. Her professional profile states that she has specialized in chronic illness management since 1995, working with people facing chronic pain, cardiac, pulmonary and neurological conditions while also supporting caregivers.

Dr. Katz-Levin's educational background began with studies in medical physiology. Physiology provides a foundation for understanding how body systems function and respond to illness. She subsequently trained in psychology, becoming licensed as a Clinical Psychologist in South Africa in 2002, according to her professional biography. This combination of physiological and psychological perspectives would become central to her later work.

Dr. Katz-Levin later completed a PhD in the United States with concentrations in Health Psychology and Neuropsychology. Health psychology examines relationships among behavior, psychological factors and physical health. Neuropsychology focuses on the relationship between brain function and cognition, emotion and behavior. Together, these disciplines provide a framework for understanding patients whose medical conditions have psychological or cognitive consequences.

Long-term illness can require patients to make repeated adjustments. Treatment schedules, symptoms, changes in physical ability, uncertainty and dependence on health systems can all influence quality of life. Dr. Katz-Levin's work in chronic illness management emphasizes psychoeducation and active patient participation. Rather than viewing patients simply as recipients of treatment, this approach helps them better understand their conditions and participate in decisions and self-management where appropriate.

Chronic illness affects families as well as patients. Caregivers may coordinate appointments, provide emotional support, manage day-to-day needs and make difficult decisions. Dr. Katz-Levin's professional work includes caregiver support, including psychoeducational programming for families affected by dementia and other chronic conditions. Her published work also includes a chapter addressing caregiver burden and well-being among those caring for people living with autism spectrum disorders.

Dr. Katz-Levin's areas of expertise include dementia care and aging-brain education. Her research record also includes work involving neuropsychological assessment. In 2021, she was a co-author of a peer-reviewed study in the Journal of Alzheimer's Disease examining auditory naming performance in people with dementia and other cognitive conditions. The research evaluated whether auditory naming measures could help reveal word-finding difficulties that might not be detected as effectively through conventional visual naming measures.

At New York Medical College, Dr. Katz-Levin serves in a leadership role focused on patient care within medical education. Her work places patient experience directly within the training of future physicians. Medical students must learn anatomy, physiology, diagnostics and treatment, but patient-centered medicine also requires communication, empathy and an understanding of how illness affects a person's life. Dr. Katz-Levin's work helps connect those dimensions.

Education has been a major theme throughout Dr. Katz-Levin's career. She has taught health care professionals nationally and internationally. She also teaches subjects including neuropsychology, health psychology and psychological assessment within doctoral clinical psychology education. This teaching connects research and clinical experience with the preparation of future professionals.

Clinical communication is not simply a matter of courtesy. Patients need to understand diagnoses, treatment choices and follow-up instructions. Clinicians need accurate information about symptoms, concerns and barriers to care. Dr. Katz-Levin's career has included leadership in communication and interprofessional skills, reinforcing the importance of communication as a clinical competency.

Earlier in her career, Dr. Katz-Levin led Training and Development for Janssen-Cilag Pharmaceuticals, according to her professional profile. The role expanded her educational work into the pharmaceutical sector. It also added another perspective on how clinical information is communicated to professionals within the broader health care system.

Dr. Katz-Levin's expertise extends to trauma-informed care, including child, adult and elder abuse. Trauma-informed health care recognizes that past or ongoing traumatic experiences can affect how a person experiences medical environments, communicates with professionals and responds to treatment. This perspective encourages clinicians to approach patients with sensitivity while avoiding assumptions about what they may have experienced.

Dr. Katz-Levin served on the Commission to Combat Domestic Violence for the New York Mayor's Office, according to her professional biography. Her work contributed to the development of guidance for medical providers caring for domestic-violence patients within different cultural contexts. The experience reflects an important theme in her career: health care does not occur in isolation from a patient's social environment.

Culture can influence how people understand illness, family responsibilities, mental health and interactions with clinicians. Health professionals therefore benefit from recognizing that communication strategies cannot always be identical for every patient. Dr. Katz-Levin's work involving domestic-violence care within cultural contexts highlights the importance of combining clinical knowledge with awareness of patients' lived realities.

Dr. Katz-Levin directs research focused on physical and mental health, with particular interest in chronic illness. Her work has generated scholarly publications across subjects including neuropsychological assessment, chronic illness, caregiver support and Long COVID. Research allows clinicians and educators to move beyond assumptions and examine patterns systematically. For Dr. Katz-Levin, that research remains closely connected with patient experience.

Long COVID has created new questions about the relationship between physical symptoms, cognition and psychological well-being. A 2025 publication co-authored by Dr. Katz-Levin examined associations among Long COVID, neurological symptoms and anxiety. The study addressed symptoms including brain fog and difficulty concentrating and explored their relationship with psychological distress. This work fits naturally within her longstanding interests in chronic illness, neuropsychology and health psychology.

Dr. Katz-Levin has also presented work examining neurological manifestations and psychological factors associated with Long COVID. A 2025 international conference program identified her as Director of Patient Care for the MD Program at New York Medical College and described research involving persistent cognitive symptoms such as brain fog, concentration difficulties and word-finding problems. The work illustrates how her research continues to address emerging health challenges.

Dr. Katz-Levin has also written about the well-being of health care professionals. In an article for the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation, she addressed the necessity of self-care among professionals working in demanding health environments. The topic extends her interest in caregiver well-being to the professionals who provide care. Sustainable patient care depends in part on a workforce capable of maintaining its own health and functioning.

Her work has included cardiac and pulmonary health as well as chronic pain and neurological concerns. She has held leadership involvement within professional rehabilitation communities and serves on the board of the New York State Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation, according to her current professional profile. These connections reinforce the multidisciplinary nature of her career.

Another area of emphasis is preparing future physicians to provide inclusive care for people living with both visible and non-visible disabilities. Some disabilities are immediately apparent. Others, including chronic pain, cognitive problems and certain neurological or psychological conditions, may not be obvious to an observer. Inclusive medical education encourages clinicians to avoid judging a patient's needs solely on appearance.

Psychoeducation is central to Dr. Katz-Levin's approach. Patients who understand their conditions may be better equipped to ask informed questions, communicate concerns and participate in their care. This does not replace professional medical guidance. Instead, it supports a relationship in which the patient is treated as an informed participant rather than a passive observer.

Dr. Katz-Levin's professional biography places strong emphasis on compassion and respect. These qualities can sound simple, but they have practical significance. A patient who feels dismissed may withhold information. A caregiver who feels overwhelmed may struggle to implement a complicated plan. A clinician who listens carefully may gain information that changes the understanding of a case. Patient-centered care therefore combines technical knowledge with human connection.

Dr. Katz-Levin's career crosses boundaries among medicine, psychology, education and research. She has worked with chronic illness patients and caregivers. She has taught health professionals. She has contributed to trauma and domestic-violence initiatives. She has conducted and published research. She now helps shape patient-centered medical education at New York Medical College. Each area reinforces the others.

Dynamo Times recognizes Leanne Katz-Levin, PhD, for her contributions to health psychology, neuropsychology, chronic illness management, patient-centered medical education, research and compassionate health care.

About New York Medical College

Leanne Katz-Levin, PhD, is a health care educator, clinical psychologist, researcher and patient-care leader. Her current professional profile identifies her as Director of Patient Care for the School of Medicine at New York Medical College and Assistant Professor in the Department of Medicine. Her areas of specialization include chronic illness management, cardiac and pulmonary care, chronic pain, neurological concerns, caregiver support, dementia, trauma, domestic violence, psychoeducation and psychological assessment. She completed studies in medical physiology, became licensed as a clinical psychologist in South Africa in 2002 and later earned a PhD in the United States with concentrations in Health Psychology and Neuropsychology.
